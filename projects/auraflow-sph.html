AuraFlow: a GPU SPH solver for violent free-surface flow

Weakly compressible smoothed-particle hydrodynamics in 2D and 3D, written on NVIDIA Warp and run on a laptop GPU. It is validated against two standard dam-break experiments, differentiable end to end, and fast enough to generate training data for surrogates and reinforcement learning.

Why build it

Commercial and open SPH codes are fast, but they are closed to the things I wanted to try: taking gradients through a simulation, generating thousands of consistent runs for a surrogate, and training a controller inside the solver. AuraFlow started in JAX; a benchmark showed NVIDIA Warp running the same kernels 162–375 times faster than the CPU path, so from v0.2 on it is a Warp-only GPU engine. Each version is frozen with its own audit and roadmap before the next one starts.

What is in it

  • Physics chosen by validation. δ-SPH with modified dynamic boundary conditions in 2D; Riemann SPH with density re-initialisation and Adami walls in 3D, because that combination passed the 3D gates and the 2D one did not.
  • Open boundaries. A stream tunnel with inflow and outflow buffers (after Tafuni 2018) holds a body in a steady stream for as long as needed. An empty tunnel stays uniform within 0.22% over ten flow-throughs.
  • Speed work, measured. Own sorted cell lists, separate fluid and wall derivative passes and CUDA-graph step blocks cut the 3D step time 2.46×. A thermal guard keeps the laptop GPU under 87 °C.
  • Differentiable. Gradients of a final-state quantity with respect to viscosity, δ, α, gravity and initial velocities. The forward pass is bit-identical to the production solver; gradients agree with finite differences within 5%, and an inverse run recovers gravity in four gradient evaluations.
  • Batched runs. Sixteen small cases share one launch, each bit-identical to its solo run, for 9.3× the throughput. A data factory turns a YAML spec into a resumable dataset with provenance per row.
  • Learning on top. A Gaussian-process surrogate with active learning and conformal 90% intervals; a Transolver-style field surrogate trained on 320 dam breaks that beats nearest-neighbour lookup by 36–47%; reinforcement learning with SAC.

Tolerances were declared before each run. The 2D case was checked at three resolutions and with five-member ensembles, which showed that levels and window-averaged pressures are robust while raw impact peaks are chaotic: a 10-4 dx perturbation moves one peak by a factor of almost three.

What does not pass yet. Raw impact peak pressures are too high, a known weakly-compressible water-hammer effect; sloshing peaks come out 2–4× the experimental band although their timing is right. Cases added after v0.5 are correctness-tested but their full validation is scheduled for v1.0.

Reinforcement learning inside the solver

After a literature review (DRLinSPH, FluidGym, HydroGym), the recipe that worked was SAC acting as a residual on a linear expert, with smoothed sensors and free-surface observations, trained on batched tanks.

On a rotating cylinder at Re 100 (HydroGym conventions), SAC trained on a coarse grid and fine-tuned at D/dx 20 reduced drag by 5.8% and lift by 50%. The published DRLinSPH forced-baffle benchmark is built but not reproduced yet; small-amplitude forced sloshing is a v1.0 item.

Lessons

  • Declare the tolerance before the run. One early pressure "near miss" turned out to be a sensor that went dry; fixed gates made that visible.
  • Chaotic quantities are not learnable targets. Post-impact pressure varies more between ensemble members than between designs, so the surrogates predict impact time and levels instead.
  • Measure before optimising. Memory reordering bought 7%; splitting the fluid and wall passes bought 2×.
  • Not every idea survives. A graded adaptive-resolution scheme was stable in a steady stream but not with a free surface near walls, and was removed in v0.9.
