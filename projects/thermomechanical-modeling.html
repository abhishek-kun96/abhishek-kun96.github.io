Case study · Gleeble 3500 + Quantitative metallography · TUM

Thermal cycles, microstructure & the limits of Kirkaldy–Li HAZ models

Gleeble 3500 simulation of S355J2C+C heat-affected zones, quantitative metallography validated against synthetic Voronoi oracles, an audit of semi-empirical austenite-decomposition modelling, and a model-free microstructural hardness bound.

Context
TUM lab experiments + independent v2.1 modelling & metallography audit (October 2026)
Role
Analysis, software, quantitative metallography & model validation. The Gleeble cycles were run by the lab-course group (“Thermomechanical material behaviour”, TUM), and some micrographs were contributed by fellow course members.
Stack
Gleeble 3500, Vickers HV1, Leica optical microscopy, Python, scikit-image, SciPy, pytest (29 tests)

In short

  • What: three Gleeble 3500 weld-HAZ cycles on S355J2C+C steel (232.1, 208.4 and 164.3 HV1), re-analysed from the raw instrument files, with automated, oracle-tested metallography.
  • What went wrong before: an earlier “model within 10 %” match came from two input errors cancelling out. With the measured cooling curve, the same model predicts 286–323 HV.
  • Outcome: a model-free bound shows no ferrite–pearlite structure in this steel reaches 232 HV at the measured grain size (≈ 200 HV for grains ≥ 3 µm), so the light phase is not polygonal ferrite; and a ±39 HV Monte Carlo spread shows one hardness value cannot validate such a model.

The validation question

Semi-empirical Kirkaldy–Li austenite-decomposition models coupled with Maynier hardness relations are widely used to predict weld heat-affected-zone (HAZ) hardness from a thermal history T(t) and steel composition. Yet in practice they are almost always checked against a single scalar — bulk Vickers hardness — while key inputs (exact heat carbon content, prior-austenite grain size) are only known within standard brackets.

Using three Gleeble 3500 cycles on cold-drawn S355J2C+C structural steel (DIN EN 10277), this study asks what validation a Kirkaldy-type model actually requires before its output can be trusted, and what an independent, model-free microstructural bound says about the phase identity of a rapidly cooled fine-grained HAZ.

Measured thermal histories & HV1 hardness

Thermocouple logs (.d0x, 100 Hz) and raw Vickers HV1 indents (22–23 indents per specimen across four cross-section positions) were re-analysed directly from the instrument files:

232.1 ± 8.4
Single Cycle A (HV1, n = 22): peak 1025.5 °C (FGHAZ), t8/5 = 6.90 s, φ700 = 51.5 °C/s
208.4 ± 15.4
Multi Cycle (HV1, n = 23): 8 peaks (1358 → 596 °C, CGHAZ → SCHAZ), t8/5 = 6.3 → 13.6 s
164.3 ± 2.4
Single A + HT (HV1, n = 23): 900 °C / 600 s hold, slow cool t8/5 ≈ 150 s, φ700 = 2.0 °C/s
p = 0.0017
Multi Cycle spatial position effect (Holm-adjusted ANOVA, Δ = 25.3 HV across HAZ sub-zones)

Pairwise Welch t-tests confirm large differences between the three specimens (|d| ≥ 1.9, pHolm < 10−6). Within a specimen, indent position is statistically significant only for the Multi Cycle (pHolm = 0.0017; Single A and A+HT both give pHolm = 1.0), consistent with a spatial superposition of differently re-austenitised and tempered zones.

Exposing a compensating error in the prior model application

An earlier application of the open-source Li (1998) / Maynier (1978) code reported 208 HV for Single Cycle A — seemingly within 10% of the measured 232.1 HV1. Re-examining the inputs revealed two errors acting in opposite directions: the cooling branch had been entered as a constant linear ramp of 7.37 °C/s (the average from peak to 80 °C, giving t8/5 = 40.7 s instead of the measured 6.90 s) alongside the DIN EN 10277 maximum composition (0.20 wt% C).

Replacing only the linear ramp with the measured Gleeble cooling curve moves the uncorrected prediction from 208 HV to 286–323 HV (78–92% bainite across ASTM G 8–12). The earlier agreement was compensating error, not validation.

Code audit, numerical sensitivity & Monte Carlo propagation

  • Two solver corrections. Auditing the open-source implementation against Li et al. (1998) identified and fixed two deviations: (1) ferrite reaction progress XF is now capped by the lever-rule equilibrium fraction Feq(T) so slow cooling forms pearlite (84% ferrite / 16% pearlite on A+HT at 0.14 wt% C, whereas the uncorrected code consumed 100% of austenite as ferrite and never formed pearlite); and (2) the 700 °C cooling rate φ700 is extracted by a least-squares slope over 650–750 °C rather than ill-posed inverse interpolation on noisy thermocouple data.
  • Discretisation uncertainty. Sweeping the cooling-curve sampling stride from 10 to 100 samples changes the corrected model's predicted hardness by 9.2 HV peak-to-peak (σ = 3.1 HV) — comparable to the experimental indent scatter (8.4 HV).
  • Monte Carlo over admissible input brackets (N = 250). Because no mill certificate or spark-OES analysis was available, propagating the admissible brackets (C 0.08–0.15, Mn 1.00–1.60, Si 0.10–0.55, Cu 0–0.55 wt%, prior-austenite ASTM G 9–12) yields 182 ± 39 HV for Single Cycle A (5th–95th percentile 134–246 HV, z = −1.28 against 232.1 HV) and 145 ± 20 HV for A+HT. Standardised regression coefficients rank Mn (β = 0.72) and C (β = 0.45) ahead of grain size (β = −0.31). When the model's output spread over unconstrained inputs is ±39 HV, matching a single hardness number within 10% does not test the kinetics.

Two-observable consistency test

Adding a second observable from quantitative metallography changes the test completely. Across all 32 points of the admissible (C, G, Mn/Si/Cu) grid on Single Cycle A, if the optically dark constituent (0.103 ± 0.086) is identified with the model's non-ferrite fraction, 0 of 32 combinations fall within 1σ of both hardness and dark fraction (minimum Mahalanobis distance 5.3, used as a screening statistic with the dark-fraction threshold band; the model is 80 HV too soft at the measured dark fraction).

Quantitative metallography & model-free Gladman–Pickering bound

Optical micrographs (2% nital, calibrated from Leica .xlif pixel metadata at 500× and 1000×) were measured with an automated pipeline: Otsu thresholding with a 0.4 µm morphological opening for the dark constituent (±0.04 grey-level sensitivity band) and black top-hat filtering + skeletonisation for ASTM E112 lineal intercepts. Before touching real micrographs, both routines were verified against synthetic Poisson–Voronoi tessellations clipped with the Liang–Barsky algorithm (analytic stereological intercept ℓ̄ = πA / 2Ltotal recovered within 10%; dark-disc area recovered within 0.02). The measured mean intercepts are 7.8 µm (ASTM G ≈ 10.7) in the base metal, 7.0 µm in Single Cycle A, and 6.3 µm in A+HT (magnification-invariant within 7–8% between 500× and 1000×).

Without invoking Li kinetics or Maynier relations, the Gladman–Pickering + Hall–Petch relation bounds any near-equilibrium ferrite–pearlite aggregate in this steel to 158 ± 11 HV (5th–95th percentile 139–178 HV) at the measured grain size (6.0–8.1 µm), and to ≈ 200 HV for all grain sizes d ≥ 3 µm (reaching 232 HV only at the 1.0 µm range edge under maximum Mn, Si, and pearlite). This independently rules out polygonal ferrite as the light-etching matrix in Single Cycle A. Published data — Gyura et al. (2021) on flame-straightened S355J2+N and Górka (2016) on simulated HAZs at t8/5 < 10 s (230–265 HV1) — are consistent with, but do not demonstrate, a bainitic transformation product. Because granular bainite and polygonal ferrite have nearly indistinguishable nano-hardness (2.7 vs 2.6 GPa, Jentner et al. 2023), EBSD kernel-average misorientation (KAM > 3°) is identified as the decisive discriminating test.

Model-free carbon bound, austenite homogenisation & negative result

  • Two independent model-free routes to carbon content. On the equilibrium A+HT specimen, inverting the optical dark fraction via the lever rule gives a 90% interval of 0.067–0.123 wt% C (central 0.090 wt%), while inverting the measured hardness through the Gladman–Pickering relation gives 0.048–0.268 wt% C (central 0.159 wt%). Their overlap (0.067–0.123 wt%) bounds the unmeasured heat carbon well below the 0.20 wt% DIN EN 10277 maximum without using the transformation model.
  • 1D Ågren carbon homogenisation check. Solving the non-isothermal 1D finite-volume diffusion equation with Ågren's concentration-dependent diffusivity along the measured Single Cycle A ramp (419 °C/s heating) shows that for the observed ferrite/pearlite island repeat distance λ = 15–25 µm, austenite is homogeneous to within a few hundredths of a wt% C before cooling below Ae3 (significant inhomogeneity requires λ ≥ 40 µm). Incomplete homogenisation is therefore rejected as the cause of the Single Cycle A hardness.
  • Negative result reported in full. A purely optical morphology discriminator based on the structure-tensor coherence of grain-boundary skeletons was tested as a zero-cost substitute for a tint etch or EBSD, but failed its own synthetic Voronoi equiaxed control (coherence 0.41–0.91 across seed densities; Welch p = 0.20 between Single A and polygonal controls) and was rejected.
